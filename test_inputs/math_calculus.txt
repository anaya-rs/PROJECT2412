Calculus is the mathematical study of continuous change, divided into two main branches: differential calculus and integral calculus. Developed independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the 17th century, calculus has become fundamental to modern mathematics, science, and engineering.

Differential calculus focuses on the concept of the derivative, which represents the rate of change of a function. The derivative f'(x) of a function f(x) at a point x is defined as the limit of the difference quotient as the interval approaches zero: f'(x) = lim(h→0) [f(x+h) - f(x)]/h. Geometrically, the derivative represents the slope of the tangent line to the function's graph at that point.

The process of finding derivatives is called differentiation. Basic differentiation rules include the power rule: d/dx(x^n) = nx^(n-1), the product rule: d/dx(fg) = f'g + fg', and the chain rule for composite functions: d/dx(f(g(x))) = f'(g(x)) · g'(x). These rules allow us to find derivatives of complex functions built from simpler ones.

Applications of differential calculus include finding maximum and minimum values of functions (optimization problems), determining velocity and acceleration in physics, analyzing rates of change in economics and biology, and understanding the behavior of functions near critical points.

Integral calculus deals with the concept of the integral, which represents the accumulation of quantities. The definite integral ∫[a,b] f(x)dx represents the area under the curve f(x) from x=a to x=b. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects differentiation and integration, showing that they are inverse operations.

The process of finding integrals is called integration. Basic integration techniques include substitution, integration by parts, and partial fractions. The antiderivative F(x) of a function f(x) satisfies F'(x) = f(x), and indefinite integrals represent families of antiderivatives plus a constant of integration.

Applications of integral calculus include calculating areas and volumes, determining displacement from velocity, finding work done by variable forces, computing probabilities in statistics, and solving differential equations that model real-world phenomena.

Multivariable calculus extends these concepts to functions of several variables, introducing partial derivatives, multiple integrals, and vector calculus. This branch is essential for understanding fields like electromagnetism, fluid dynamics, and optimization in higher dimensions.

Calculus provides the mathematical foundation for understanding change and motion in the physical world, making it indispensable for physics, engineering, economics, computer graphics, machine learning, and many other fields that deal with continuous systems and rates of change.
